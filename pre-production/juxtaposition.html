1.6

Juxtaposition in Mise-en-Scène

Purpose

This assignment invites you to analyze how filmmakers use juxtaposition in mise-en-scène to create meaning without explicitly spelling it out for the audience. Juxtaposition literally means placing two or more contrasting elements side by side so the viewer generates meaning out of the friction between them.

The Concept

Cinema thrives on showing rather than telling. When you place contrasting costumes, environments, or props in the same frame, the visual collision does the heavy lifting:

  • Character Appearance & Wardrobe: Contrasting textures, colors, or styles (tattered workwear vs. luxury suit).
  • Character vs. Environment: Placing an outsider in a rigid, uniform architectural setting.
  • Props & Object Contrast: Placing contradictory objects in the same space to reveal hypocrisy, irony, or subtext.

Films to Choose From

Analyze juxtaposition in mise-en-scène in a scene from one of these films:

  • Rental Family, Hikari (2025)
  • Outrage, Takeshi Kitano (2010)
  • Tampopo, Juzo Itami (1985)
  • Rashomon, Akira Kurosawa (1950)

Prompts

Prompt 1: The Visual Collision

Goal: Identify the specific scene and timestamp where two contrasting physical elements share the frame.

Ask Yourself:

  • What are the two contrasting elements? (Wardrobe, architecture, props, color palette?)
  • How does the frame arrange them? Side by side, foreground vs. background, centered vs. marginal?
  • Is the contrast visual (color, texture, scale) or contextual (social status, cultural code)?
Prompt 2: The Unspoken Information

Goal: Explain what the visual contrast reveals without a single line of dialogue.

Ask Yourself:

  • What does the contrast tell us about status, power, or emotional state?
  • Could the same information be delivered through dialogue? Why did the filmmaker choose not to?
  • Does the juxtaposition create irony, empathy, or tension?
Prompt 3: Production Application

Goal: Translate the technique into your own Tokyo-based production plan.

Ask Yourself:

  • How can you use contrasting physical elements in real Tokyo locations (modern transit vs. historic shrines, uniform vs. casual) to tell a story visually?
  • What single frame could communicate an entire power dynamic without dialogue?
  • What props or wardrobe choices would you bring to create the strongest contrast?

Textbook Contribution

If your analysis reveals how the visual collision generates meaning, it belongs in the book.

  • The Contrast: What two elements were placed in friction?
  • The Meaning: What did the audience generate from the collision?
  • The Application: How can the technique be replicated in a Tokyo production?

Deliverables

  • Length: 500+ words. Journals are short, which fools people into thinking they are casual. They are not. The compression is the difficulty, so every sentence has to earn its place.
  • Film: one approved film from the list above. An unapproved film is a zero, no matter how good the writing is.
  • Sources: none required. The film itself is your evidence.
  • Citations: an exact MLA timestamp on every claim, plus a Works Cited entry for the film. A timestamp works like a page number in an essay about a novel. It tells your reader exactly where to look so they can check your claim against the film themselves. If the two contrasting elements appear at different moments, write a timestamp for each of them.
  • Vocabulary: the terminology of this module (juxtaposition, mise-en-scène, visual collision, contrast, wardrobe, props, character vs. environment).

How to Write This Journal

The Core Rule

Every claim does three things at once:

  • Names the technique in this module's vocabulary: juxtaposition, mise-en-scène, visual collision, contrast, wardrobe, props.
  • Locates it at an exact second in MLA form: (00:14:46).
  • Explains what it does to the meaning of the film, or to the viewer.

No term is an impression. No timestamp is a guess. No explanation is a label. After each piece of evidence, ask so what?

MLA Timestamps

A timestamp gives the hour, the minute, and the second, each field padded with a leading zero, separated by colons, and enclosed in parentheses: (00:14:46). You cite one second rather than a range.

Incorrect Correct Why
(00:14:00 to 00:15:30)(00:14:46)That covers ninety seconds. Give the one where it happens.
at (00:14:46)(00:14:46)Drop the word. The parentheses already point.
(14:46)(00:14:46)Keep the hour, even when it is zero.
(1:31:30)(01:31:30)Each field is two digits.
(00:01:37:04)(00:01:37)That fourth number is frames. Leave timecode in your edit suite.
(00:15:00) for a moment at (00:14:46)(00:14:46)Round it off and you point at the wrong moment.
around (00:48:00)(00:48:12)If you are not sure, go back and look.

Accuracy

If you write "luxury suit vs. tattered workwear (00:14:46)" and that second shows both characters in similar clothing, the claim fails. Go to the second, look at the screen, and name what is there. Describe what is actually in the frame, including wardrobe, props, and spatial arrangement.

Minimums

  • Form, not plot. "The rich man and the poor man argue" is plot. "The wide shot (00:22:04) places the suited executive in the upper third of the frame and the day laborer in the lower third, so the spatial hierarchy mirrors the economic one" is form.
  • Claims, not chronology. Group by idea, not first scene to last.
  • A Works Cited entry in MLA 9: Title of the Film. Directed by Firstname Lastname, Production Company, Year.

Evaluation Criteria

  • Content — 25. Accurate terms, accurate claims, depth past description.
  • Structure — 25. The introduction narrows to a thesis; the analysis proves it and closes.
  • Voice and Mechanics — 25. Grammar, register, sentence control, correct header.
  • Documentation — 5. Timestamp format, Works Cited, italicized titles.

A malformed timestamp costs Documentation. A missing one costs Content, which is worth five times as much.
