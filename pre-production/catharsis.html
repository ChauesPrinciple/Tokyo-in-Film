1.6

Catharsis in Film

Purpose

This assignment invites you to analyze how filmmakers construct catharsis, the emotional release at the climax or conclusion of a narrative. Using Seven Samurai (1954) as our reference example, you will examine how setup, stakes, and payoff converge to create powerful emotional impact.

Seven Samurai (1954), Akira Kurosawa

The Release

Catharsis is the emotional payoff at the end of sustained narrative tension. In Seven Samurai, Kurosawa sustains that tension for nearly three hours. We watch the farmers' desperate plight, the tedious recruitment of the samurai, and the endless, muddy tension of the defense. We invest in these characters. We worry for them.

Then, the release. It does not come with the final sword stroke of the battle. It comes in the quiet aftermath. The surviving samurai stand in front of the graves of their fallen friends. The music swells, not with a martial triumph, but with a somber, grounding melody. Below them, the villagers are singing, planting rice, returning to the cycle of life that the samurai fought to protect but can never be part of.

Kambei says, "The victory belongs to those peasants. Not to us." That is the cathartic moment. It creates a complex emotional cleanse, relief that the village is safe, mixed with the melancholy realization of the samurai's displacement. It purges the fear and tension of the battle, replacing it with a profound, bittersweet clarity.

How it Works

  • The Build-Up: The relentless pressure of the bandits' impending attack and the accumulation of individual character moments.
  • The Release: The stillness after the violence, where the cost is tallied.
  • The Transformation: We move from fear for their survival to pity for their isolation. We leave the film feeling something larger than just "the good guys won."

Films to Choose From

Analyze cathartic structure in a scene from one of these films:

  • 13 Assassins, Takashi Miike
  • Kubi, Takeshi Kitano
  • Godzilla Minus One, Takashi Yamazaki
  • Chainsaw Man: Reze Arc, Tatsuya Yoshihara

Prompts

Prompt 1: The Setup

Goal: Show us the build-up.

Ask Yourself:

  • What is the film making us wait for?
  • How does the pressure mount?
  • Where are the "false summits"?

Example: In Seven Samurai, every small victory is followed by a bigger threat.

Prompt 2: The Withholding

Goal: Identify what the film refuses to give you.

Ask Yourself:

  • What is kept just out of reach?
  • How does this create dread?
  • Why wait?

Example: Seven Samurai never lets us feel safe until the very end.

Prompt 3: The Release

Goal: Break down the moment of release.

Ask Yourself:

  • Victory or tragedy?
  • Is it earned?
  • How do you feel at the end?

Example: Kambei's line about the peasants winning is the release.

Textbook Contribution

If your analysis cuts to the core of how the film works, we will put it in the book. Do not just summarize the plot. Show us the mechanics.

  • The Mechanics of Delay: How did the film make us wait?
  • The Payoff: Why was the release satisfying?
  • The Aftermath: What emotional residue does the film leave?

Deliverables

  • Length: 500+ words. Journals are short, which fools people into thinking they are casual. They are not. The compression is the difficulty, so every sentence has to earn its place.
  • Film: one approved film from the list above. An unapproved film is a zero, no matter how good the writing is.
  • Sources: none required. The film itself is your evidence.
  • Citations: exact MLA timestamps on every claim, plus a Works Cited entry for the film.
  • Vocabulary: the terminology of this module (catharsis, setup and payoff, stakes, the build and the release).

How to Write This Journal

The Core Rule

Every claim does three things at once:

  • Names the technique in this module's vocabulary: catharsis, setup and payoff, stakes, the build, the release.
  • Locates it at an exact second in MLA form: (00:14:46).
  • Explains what it does to the meaning of the film, or to the viewer.

No term is an impression. No timestamp is a guess. No explanation is a label. After each piece of evidence, ask so what?

MLA Timestamps

Your timestamp is how your reader gets to your evidence. Three fields, hours first, two digits each, colons between them, the whole thing in parentheses: (00:14:46). That is the entire format. Every mistake below is a variation on one sin, which is leaving the finding to your reader.

  • A range. (00:14:00 to 00:15:30) hands your reader ninety seconds of film and wishes them luck. Somewhere in there is your evidence. They will settle on a moment, it will not be your moment, and they will grade the one they found. Name the second.
  • The word "at." The parentheses already said it. Drop it and write (00:14:46).
  • A missing hour. (14:46) could be fourteen minutes into the film or fourteen hours into it. You know which you meant. Your reader does not. Keep the zero: (00:14:46).
  • A missing leading zero. (1:31:30) should be (01:31:30). Two digits per field, every time, no exceptions for the small numbers.
  • A fourth field. (00:01:37:04) is timecode, and that last pair counts frames. Wonderful in your editing software. Useless in an essay, where your reader is watching a stream that cannot count frames and would not thank you for trying. Write (00:01:37).
  • A rounded number. If it happens at (00:14:46), do not write (00:15:00). Fourteen seconds is three or four shots. You have sent your reader somewhere your evidence is not, and told them it was the right place.
  • A hedge. "Around," "about," "roughly," "somewhere near." Each one announces that you did not go back and look. (Your reader can hear it.) Commit to the second: (00:48:12).

If you cannot find the exact second, you have not watched the moment closely enough to write about it yet. Scrub back, land on it, and read the number off the player.

Accuracy

If you write "close-up (00:14:46)" and that second is a wide shot, the claim fails. Go to the second, look at the screen, and name what is there. Describe what is actually in the frame and on the soundtrack.

Minimums

  • An approved film. Anything else is a zero.
  • This module's terminology. A catharsis journal analyzes catharsis.
  • An exact timestamp on every claim. These are your evidence.
  • Form, not plot. "The protagonist runs through the rain" is plot. "The tracking shot (00:22:04) holds the same distance from him for the entire run, so we are not watching him flee; we are fleeing with him" is form.
  • Claims, not chronology. Group by idea, not first scene to last.
  • A Works Cited entry in MLA 9: Title of the Film. Directed by Firstname Lastname, Production Company, Year.

Evaluation Criteria

  • Content — 25. Accurate terms, accurate claims, depth past description.
  • Structure — 25. The introduction narrows to a thesis; the analysis proves it and closes.
  • Voice and Mechanics — 25. Grammar, register, sentence control, correct header.
  • Documentation — 5. Timestamp format, Works Cited, italicized titles.

A malformed timestamp costs Documentation. A missing one costs Content, which is worth five times as much.
