2.                          TITLE CARDS (BLACK)

CARD 1: In 2053, Babel Aerospace sent the LONGSHOT past the heliopause -- the outer boundary of the Sun's influence, fifteen hundred astronomical units from Earth. Five humans, further than anything living had ever been.

CARD 2: Their drive could reach that distance in weeks. Their instruments could not navigate it. A fusion torch without aim is a torch with no use. For that they carried a cognitive relay: a four-hundred-and-twelve-electrode interface that read trained cortical signals from calibrated pilots and translated them into course corrections. Safe return required three calibrated pilots and an uncompromised relay.

CARD 3: Six months before launch, the deep-space telescope array at Lagrange 5 flagged a stellar-decay pattern in the outer Oort cloud that matched no known model. Babel assembled a crew to observe it. The mission parameters said observe.

INT. LONGSHOT -- BRIDGE -- DAY CYCLE 47 (0:15)

Black. Then the sound of the ship -- a low continuous frequency, the kind that lives in the chest. Recycled air through vents designed for function, not comfort.

The LONGSHOT is fifty meters of carbon-nanotube composite and deuterium-cycle fusion drive. On the bulkhead above the bridge entrance: a small brass commissioning plate. BABEL AEROSPACE -- LONGSHOT -- FIRST DEEP-SPACE PROGRAM. The brass is already tarnishing. Fifteen hundred astronomical units from Earth. Eight and a half light-days out. No human has ever been half this far.

The bridge has no windows. Eight screens across a curved console. Their light is the only warm thing in the room.

NAKAMURA has been awake since before the day cycle began. Sleeves pushed to the forearms. A kanzashi pin holds her hair back. Taped inside her upper panel: a photograph of a boy, maybe twelve. She is not looking at it.

Three screens. Leftmost: the decay feed. Middle: her eight-year-old theoretical work. Rightmost: a file from her personal archive, dated three years before launch, timestamped 03:47. She has not opened it.

MARSHALL enters at 06:00 exactly. Checks navigation, power, crew, life support. The anomaly feed last.

                         MARSHALL

                         NAKAMURA

                         MARSHALL
               Costa's theory.

                         NAKAMURA

INT. LONGSHOT -- SECONDARY ARRAY ROOM -- CONTINUOUS (1:00)

Smaller than a large closet. One chair, bolted. Equipment on all four walls.

The tone shifts slightly. Tanaka's hand moves to the pin on his lapel. He is not aware that it moved.

INT. LONGSHOT -- VARIOUS -- DAY CYCLE 47 (3:00-5:30)

Afternoon. Each moment held long enough to breathe.

NAKAMURA at her station. The file from three years ago unopened. She builds a bridge between her published work and the sector-nine data. The bridge fails at the same point. She deletes. TANAKA in the array room. Door mostly closed. Through the gap: the sound of him speaking, not to anyone on the ship. Words not distinct. The tone careful, precise -- a student speaking to a teacher they revere.

COSTA in the common area, timeline extended. She adds today's red mark. Steps back. Looks at the distance between red and black on the most recent date. Nearly overlapping. She sits in the folding chair doing nothing. Then picks up the pen.

CARTER in the corridor. Not going anywhere. Standing in the middle, notebook open, writing quickly. He writes the same word three times. Tears the page out. Folds it. Puts it in his pocket.

MARSHALL in his quarters. Door closed. On his desk: mission parameters, pre-launch psychological assessments, a photograph he is not looking at. Seven people in flight gear. Six smiling. Marshall not. He is looking at the camera like something he is trying to memorize.

INT. WHITE ROOM -- DREAM -- NIGHT CYCLE 51 (11:00)

The five crew members in their uniforms, standing in a white room with no visible walls. Nakamura with her collar open. Marshall sitting at attention even in a dream. Tanaka's headphones around his neck. Costa without her pen. Carter without his notebook.

               You've been on a hook.

                         TANAKA

He is routing the receiver-array feed into the relay's signal input. The relay is designed to take a pilot's cortex patterns and produce navigation. Tanaka is taking the sector-nine signal and feeding it into the place where a pilot would be.

               You don't understand what you're stopping.

                         MARSHALL
               I understand exactly what I'm stopping.

INT. LONGSHOT -- BRIDGE -- DAY CYCLE 57 (16:30)

Morning. Costa at her data station. Adds two entries to the wall timeline. Draws a line from the relay incident to a corresponding signal spike at 23:40. She stood on her chair to draw it. It runs from the baseline to the ceiling.

INT. LONGSHOT -- BRIDGE -- CONTINUOUS (17:00)

Marshall at his station. Nakamura at hers. The relay-room door shows amber. Tanaka's quarters red. Locked.

INT. LONGSHOT -- COSTA'S QUARTERS -- NIGHT CYCLE 59 (20:00)

The wall timeline is gone. She has taken it down. The wall is bare.

On the floor: a metal wastebin. Ashes still warm. All forty-three printouts burned. The red pen lies on top of the ashes.

On the desk: her notebook, open to a fresh page. She writes. CLOSE ON:

                    INSERT -- FINAL ENTRY

           TIMELINE: DESTROYED.
           LOGS: ERASED.
           OBSERVER: REMOVING SELF.
           I DO NOT KNOW IF IT WORKS.

                    BACK TO SCENE

She caps the pen. Places it exactly parallel to the notebook's spine.

She opens the personal trauma kit every crew member was issued at launch. Removes a blister sheet of sedative ampules. Counts them. Twelve.

She draws them into the pre-loaded syringe with the steady hands of someone who does precision work for a living. She has done the math. She knows the dose.

She injects.

She folds her hands on the closed notebook. Looks at the bare wall.

She waits.

The camera stays with her.


INT. LONGSHOT -- MEDBAY -- NIGHT CYCLE 59 (20:15)

Carter in the medbay. He has been here four hours. The medbay is the brightest room on the ship -- surgical lighting, fluorescent-cold, the kind of light that makes everything look like evidence.

The notebook on the floor beside him, open to yesterday's entry. Close on the page: six numbered items in his clinical hand. Items 1 through 5 ticked -- each an event that occurred today, described before it occurred. Item 6 has no tick: CARTER. MEDBAY. 23:00. REMOVE THE INSTRUMENT. The handwriting on item 6 is different. Pressed harder, slightly larger, strokes less controlled.

He has stopped trying to remember writing it.

He closes the notebook. Opens it. Closes it.

He goes to the surgical cabinet. He is unhurried. He is not in crisis -- he is in the terrible calm of completion.

What he does takes four minutes. The surgical light does not permit euphemism: he removes his eyes with an enucleation spoon and curved surgical scissors from the ophthalmic tray. He does not scream. He makes a single controlled sound, once, and then the room is quiet.

He sits against the wall below the cabinet. He is alive. Notebook fallen beside him.

Hemostatic gel from the trauma drawer. Orbital packing -- sterile mesh into the cavities. Adhesive dressings. He works without speaking. Carter does not speak either.

INT. LONGSHOT -- COSTA'S QUARTERS -- CONTINUOUS (21:20)

Marshall knocks. No response. He keys the lock override.

The door opens.

Head up. Eyes open.

He does not close it. He does not take it with him. He leaves it open on the desk so the next person who enters this room sees what she saw.

INT. LONGSHOT -- RELAY ROOM -- DAY CYCLE 62 (23:30)

The relay room. A room of deliberate quiet -- the most sophisticated piece of equipment on the ship lives here, humming its single tone. The cognitive relay: a matte-black cylinder on its mounting, indicator lights all green.

Marshall stands in front of it with a set of manual tools. He is not a man who hesitates in front of things he has decided to do. But he gives himself a moment. He looks at the relay -- the clean panels, the green lights, the casing that shows no sign it has been a door.

Then he works.

He opens the housing. Removes the outer shielding. Disengages the primary coolant line. Pulls back the quantum-entangled crystal lattice from its mounting bracket. The crystal is the size of a fist, internally illuminated, cool to touch.

He sets it on the deck. Takes a heavy steel tool from his kit. Breaks it.

The sound: a long descending tone. The relay's operating frequency -- sixty hertz, the pitch that has lived in their chests since launch -- dropping. Fifty. Forty. At twenty hertz it passes below hearing and becomes pressure in the room. Then it is gone.

The silence that follows is a kind of silence the ship has never contained.

Marshall finishes dismantling the housing. Twenty-two minutes, as promised. When the relay is cold, he sits on the floor next to it and looks at his hands.

Nakamura appears in the doorway. They look at each other. Neither speaks.


INT. LONGSHOT -- BRIDGE -- EMERGENCY LIGHTING -- DAY CYCLE 62 (24:00)

The bridge in emergency lighting. Not dark -- the emergency lights are warm, amber, the color of a fire that isn't threatening anything. More honest than the standard lighting. The screens are still on but reading limited.

Nakamura has found a bottle. It was in the galley stores, listed as CREW ALLOCATION, PERSONAL -- someone's two-liter allowance. The label says TANAKA, YUKI in the small type. Neither of them mentions this.

She has found two cups that are not for drinking. She pours into them anyway.

Marshall sits. She sits across from him. The ship hums the atmospheric recyclers and the dying fusion drive and nothing else. The sixty-hertz tone is gone. Their chests feel the absence.

EXT. LONGSHOT -- SPACE -- CONTINUOUS

The ship from outside. Fifty meters of carbon-nanotube hull, dark against a field of stars that are no longer all there. Navigation strobes on the port and starboard nacelles cycling green-white-green, as programmed. The drive has shut down. The relay is gone. The ship is drifting at the velocity and heading it had when Marshall broke the crystal.

Through the small viewport of the observation bay -- eight inches by twelve, the only real window on the ship -- the sector-nine field. The false-color map on the bridge showed it in amber and gray. From the viewport it is just a field of stars, some dimmer than they should be. The dimming is visible to the naked eye now.

The LONGSHOT does not broadcast. The relay is gone. The line is cut. Whatever was pulling here has lost its grip on this patch of the meal. It moves on. Other regions. Other hooks.

We hold on the ship a long time. A small thing, unlit, holding its last known heading toward a region of sky that is dimming because the universe has teeth marks in it there. The eating continues. The pull has stopped on this one small piece of krill.

Inside, two people are drinking. Two are asleep. One is sitting in the dark in her quarters with her hands folded on a closed notebook.

None of them are going home.
