# Chainsaw Man: Reze Arc (2025), Tatsuya Yoshihara

## Core Thesis

Chainsaw Man: Reze Arc is a film about emotional illiteracy disguised as a shōnen action movie. Its entire grammar is built on the premise that its two leads, Denji and Reze, are fluent in violence but cannot read the language of intimacy. The film's formal genius is that it mirrors their disability: it communicates its tragedy not through eloquent dialogue but through color shifts, sound design, and the physical comedy of bodies that do not know how to touch without hurting.

Reze Arc understands that the horror of Chainsaw Man has never been the devils but the systems that manufacture them. Makima does not need to fight. She only needs to listen. The film's cinematic grammar, shifting from the warm stasis of the cafe to the neon chaos of the typhoon, from the "ting" of Reze's blasts to the silence of the rewritten menu, is a map of emotional colonization. Reze's tragedy is not that she dies, but that she learns to feel just in time to understand that feeling is a liability. The film's true monster is not the Bomb Devil. It is the blackboard menu in an empty cafe: a message of love written in a language its intended reader cannot decipher, surviving as the only proof that something real happened between two people who were never taught how to say it.

To understand why this film hits harder than standard shōnen, you need to know what it's subverting. The shōnen tradition (Naruto, My Hero Academia, Demon Slayer) is built on a promise: the protagonist's sincerity will be rewarded. Hard work, friendship, and perseverance will overcome any obstacle. Chainsaw Man's Tatsuki Fujimoto takes that promise and sets it on fire. Denji's sincerity is real, but the world does not reward it. The system (Makima, Public Safety, the devil economy) consumes sincerity like fuel. Reze Arc inherits this thesis and translates it into cinematic grammar: the warm cafe is the shōnen promise. The cold alleyway is the Fujimoto reality. The film makes you fall in love with the promise before it shows you the reality.

## The Bifurcated Grammar: Rom-Com Stasis vs. Action Kinetics

The film splits cleanly into two visual registers. The first half is shot like a grounded romantic drama, Your Name by way of a back-alley cafe. The school, the pool, the fireworks use claustrophobic compositions where shadows demonstrate volume and intimacy is framed through proximity rather than movement. Shirts ruffle, creases smooth, and the camera stays close enough to count eyelashes. This is not the kinetic, wire-assisted action grammar of Rurouni Kenshin. It is the grammar of hesitation. When Denji and Reze sit in the pool, the water does not ripple for spectacle. It holds them in a suspended, blue-tiled space where neither knows how to say what they feel.

Then the film ruptures. The transition from the school to the festival is deliberately abrupt, a jump in time that the film refuses to smooth over, because Reze's life has no continuity between her cover and her mission. The second half explodes into a sensory assault: neon color splashes, kinetic debris, and a typhoon fight where windows shatter in compositions borrowed from Mob Psycho 100. The camera, previously patient, becomes erratic, mirroring Denji's mental state. The 2D/3D hybrid that MAPPA deploys is not a budget shortcut here. It is a formal choice that makes the devil fights feel like a different medium intruding on the romance.

## Key Scenes

### The Lullaby and the Coffee Cup

Reze sings a lullaby while choking a man to death. The sound design isolates her voice, making it diegetic and intimate. Then the film cuts to Makima shaking her coffee cup in the exact same rhythm. It is a masterclass in cross-cutting: the lullaby is not Reze's private music but a signal that Makima has already heard, already claimed, already weaponized. The catharsis of the scene is poisoned in real time. (This is the film's thesis in a single cut: intimacy is always already surveilled.)

### The Pool

This is the film's emotional anchor. The water is still, the lighting is soft, and the conversation drifts to the Town Mouse and the Country Mouse. But the visual metaphor is richer than the dialogue. Denji is framed as a leaping frog in Reze's eyes, a frog in boiling water, unaware of the temperature rising. Reze, meanwhile, is the dissected frog from her backstory. The pool is supposed to be a sanctuary, but the film frames it as an aquarium where both specimens are being observed.

Reze teaches Denji to swim. The vulnerability of learning to swim mirrors the vulnerability of trust. The pool is the film at its most human: no devils, no violence, just two people in water. (And the film knows this, which is why it cannot last.)

### The Blackboard Menu

After the credits, a quick shot of the cafe menu reveals eraser marks: the text has been rewritten entirely in kana because Denji cannot read kanji. This is the film's most devastating piece of visual storytelling. It happens after Reze is gone, meaning it is a posthumous confession of care that Denji may never notice. The grammar is purely cinematic. No dialogue, no music sting, just a static shot of a text that has been altered by a hand that will never hold his again.

### The Train Hesitation

Reze approaches the train, stops, decides not to board, and walks back toward the cafe where Denji is waiting. The camera follows her in a continuous take that lasts minutes. This is the film's true climax, not the bomb fight. The action sequences are cathartic for the genre. This hesitation is cathartic for the character. She is a weapon programmed to kill, and for five minutes, she malfunctions in the direction of love.

The film offers a series of false summits before this: the underwater fight appears to end the conflict, the beach scene appears to offer resolution, Reze walking away appears to be the ending. Each time, the film resets the tension. The train hesitation is the final false summit before the real ending.

### The Kanda Cafe Sequences

The cafe sequences use symmetrical framing to communicate safety and control. Denji centered in frame means the cafe is his space. The cafe is the film's domestic anchor: the space where Denji is not Chainsaw Man, where Reze is not the Bomb Devil, where both are simply people. The film's tragedy is that this space is an illusion.

### The Murder of Reze

Reze walks back toward the cafe. Cold lighting draws a parallel to the beginning of the film. Somber piano music. The composition at the end of the alleyway gives the impression of loneliness. She increases her pace. Rack focus hones in on the cafe.

Then Makima blocks her path. She references the country mouse, city mouse metaphor. Her interpretation implies nowhere is safe to run. Close-up on Reze's face: dread. Close-up on Makima: calm, as if she has already won.

Reze strikes first. Her arm is cut off by an unseen force. She moves behind Makima. The composition layers blood, Makima, and Reze. She is attacked again, fatally through the heart. The camera pans upward to reveal Angel on the roof, spear in hand. The unseen attacker.

The lighting immediately shifts to a brighter, warmer tone, as if with Reze's death the world is returning to normal. The somber piano music remains unchanged. The final shot: 4:1 lighting on Reze's face. Her callback line: "I've never been to school either." The camera pans to Denji in the cafe with flowers.

### The Final Sequence: Theater to Alleyway

The final sequence, from the theater scene with Makima through the alleyway confrontation, is structured for Kuleshov analysis: the juxtaposition between shots generates meaning that individual frames do not carry. What do these edits reveal about Denji's emotional position at the moment of the betrayal that dialogue or performance alone could not? Everything. The cuts are the thesis.

## Comedy as Defense Mechanism

The film's humor is not relief but characterological armor. Denji's defining trait is that he just wants to get laid, a reduction of shōnen protagonist ambition to its biological minimum. The comedy comes from the gap between the epic scale of devil hunting and the pettiness of Denji's desires. When Chainsaw Man rides Beam the shark like a horse, the image is absurd, but the absurdity is Denji's only emotional register. He cannot process grief or romance, so he processes them through Looney Tunes logic. The film's violence inherits this tone: hyper-violent and simultaneously hyper-stupid, because sincerity is scarier than death.

## Catharsis and the Failure of Language

Traditional catharsis would require Denji to save Reze or Reze to escape. The film denies both. Her final line, "I've never been to school either," is not a declaration of love but an admission of shared deprivation. It reframes her entire arc not as a femme fatale's betrayal but as two orphans comparing stolen childhoods. The film's ending is a slow upward pan from Angel's final line to the rat, then to the city on the horizon. There is no victory. There is only the recognition that the country mouse and the city mouse are both being hunted by the same hawk.

The exhale is not complete joy. Denji survives but the hope for a romantic relationship is shattered. You are left with a greater sense of loss than victory. Reze isn't given the chance to change, Denji is left alone again, and the catharsis gained by Reze's choice is for naught.

## Sound Design

- The lullaby scene isolates Reze's voice, making it diegetic and intimate, then cuts to Makima shaking her coffee cup in the exact same rhythm. Sound becomes surveillance.
- Somber piano music runs through the final scene and remains unchanged after Reze's death. The score does not swell for the murder or shift for the emotional payoff. It treats the killing as inevitable, already scored.
- The piano is the film's emotional constant: the same theme at the beginning and the end, suggesting that the outcome was always written.
- The "ting" of Reze's blasts is the sonic signature of the action register, contrasted against the silence of the rewritten menu.

## Mise-en-Scène Juxtaposition

The strongest juxtaposition is the bifurcated grammar itself: rom-com stasis vs. action kinetics. The cafe is warm, symmetrical, safe. The alleyway is cold, deep, lonely. The typhoon fight is neon chaos. Reze moves from one register to the other, and the transition from symmetrical safety to asymmetric danger is the film's spatial argument: the domestic space was always provisional, always one Makima away from being destroyed. The country mouse, city mouse metaphor is the thesis: the cafe is the country, the alleyway is the city, and Makima is the border between them that no one crosses. The blackboard menu is the final juxtaposition: a message of love in an empty cafe, surviving in a language its reader cannot decode.

## Characters

- **Denji**: The protagonist. A young man who carries the Chainsaw Devil within him. His desire is not heroic but domestic: food, affection, a normal life. He is emotionally illiterate, fluent in violence but unable to read the language of intimacy. His defining trait, that he just wants to get laid, is a reduction of shōnen ambition to its biological minimum. The film treats his emotional vulnerability as more dangerous than his physical power.
- **Reze**: A girl who works at a cafe. She is the Bomb Devil. Her tenderness and violence come from the same body. The film's central question is whether the woman can override the devil. The answer is yes, but Makima kills her before it matters. She is both the leaping frog and the dissected frog: the one who does not know the water is boiling, and the one who has already been opened up and examined.
- **Makima**: Head of Special Division 4. She represents the system that owns Denji and Reze. Her calm during the murder is the film's most terrifying image of power. She does not need to fight. She only needs to listen. The lullaby-to-coffee-cup cross-cut reveals her method: she does not intercept, she monitors.
- **Aki**: A member of Public Safety who dismisses Reze's claims of love as lies.
- **Angel**: The unseen attacker who kills Reze with a spear from a rooftop. Revealed only after the fatal blow. The film's silent executioner.
- **Beam**: The shark devil Denji rides like a horse. The absurdity of the image is Denji's only emotional register.

## My Understanding

Reze Arc is a film about two people who were never taught the language of intimacy, and its grammar mirrors their disability. The first half speaks in the warm, hesitant vocabulary of romance. The second half speaks in the neon, kinetic vocabulary of violence. The transition between them is a cut, not a scene. That abruptness is the film's thesis: Reze's cover and her mission have no continuity, and the film's grammar refuses to pretend they do. The blackboard menu is the film's most devastating image because it is the only moment where someone tries to bridge the gap between these two registers. Reze rewrote the menu in kana so Denji could read it. She learned his language. He will never know. The film's deepest provocation is that the shōnen promise, that sincerity will be rewarded, is a lie. But the lie is beautiful. And the beauty is all you have.
