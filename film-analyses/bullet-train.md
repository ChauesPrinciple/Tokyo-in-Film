# Bullet Train (2022), David Leitch

## Core Thesis

Bullet Train is a film about two things at once: the impossibility of maintaining a persona in motion, and karma as an architectural inevitability. David Leitch understands that a bullet train is a space of enforced intimacy, strangers trapped in proximity, unable to leave, unable to turn around, and his formal response is to treat the vehicle as a pressure cooker for imported identities. The film's grammar is lateral and interruptive: it fights the train's forward motion with constant sideways cuts, to other cars, to hyper-saturated flashbacks, to cameo appearances that function like commercial breaks. The result is a comedy of collision, where every character's self-mythology arrives fully formed and is immediately tested by the architecture.

The karma thesis is the film's structural engine. Every throwaway detail, every comedic cutaway, every prop introduced without ceremony is a seed that will grow into a consequence. The film states it directly through Ladybug: "You put peace out into the world, you get peace back." But the thesis is not spoken. It is engineered. Every character is undone by their own weapon, lie, or game. The train itself is the thesis made spatial: a closed system where no action escapes consequence. You cannot leave without settling your account. The Shinkansen is not a setting but a karmic accelerator where cause and effect are compressed into a single journey from Tokyo to Kyoto.

The two theses are the same thesis. The humans keep dying because they cannot stop performing, and their performances are what kill them. The water bottle survives because it has no persona to lose, and because it has no karma to settle. The objects persist. The humans do not. The film's grammar of interruption, flashbacks interrupt fights, cameos interrupt plot, confined choreography interrupts every character's self-image, is the mechanism by which karma catches up. You can move laterally all you want. The train is still going forward.

To understand what Leitch is doing, you need to know the container thriller tradition he inherits. Speed (1994, Jan de Bont) trapped its characters on a bus with a bomb. Snowpiercer (2013, Bong Joon-ho) turned a train into a class allegory. Narrow Margin (1990, Peter Hyams) used a train as a sealed arena for a chase. Leitch's contribution is to make the container a comedy engine. Where Speed was suspense and Snowpiercer was allegory, Bullet Train is a joke. The train is a setup, and the punchline is that no one can get off until the karmic account is settled. Leitch also brings his background as the uncredited co-director of John Wick (2014) (Chad Stahelski got sole DGA credit) and the credited director of Atomic Blonde (2017). He knows how to shoot close-quarters combat, but here he deliberately constrains it further: the train car is too narrow for the Wick aesthetic, so the choreography becomes vertical, rotational, and absurd. The constraint produces the comedy.

## The Flashback as Comedic Speed-Bump

When The Wolf (Bad Bunny) boards the train, the film does not introduce him through dialogue or threat. It interrupts the present tense with a rapid-fire wedding massacre flashback, operatic, saturated, tragic in its imagery. Then he dies in thirty seconds. This is the film's central formal joke: it grants characters epic backstory at the speed of a trailer, then discards them before the emotion can land. The Father (Andrew Koji) gets identical treatment: his son pushed from a roof, his quest delivered in quick-cut exposition. These flashbacks are visually louder than the present (more blood, more color, more camera movement), but their velocity makes them absurd. The film is not interested in tragedy. It is interested in the trappings of tragedy, the way assassins carry origin stories like luggage they refuse to check.

The Bolivia job flashback operates on the same principle but with a structural twist: it is played as a five-second dark comedy cutaway early in the film, costumes and chaos and punchline, and you laugh and move on. But it is also a setup, foreshadowing exactly how Lemon and Tangerine operate and how the bond between them will eventually break. The flashback is simultaneously a speed-bump and a seed.

## The Object as Protagonist

In a film where human characters arrive and depart with such speed, objects achieve permanence. The water bottle, knocked over, picked up, surviving every fight, becomes the film's most consistent presence. Audiences identified it as the best "character" because it has no backstory, no motivation, no flashback. It simply persists. The briefcase operates similarly: a metallic shape passed from hand to hand, never opened, its contents irrelevant. It is a visual relay baton, a reason for the camera to keep moving. And Lemon's Thomas the Tank Engine sticker, slapped onto The Prince's clothing before his apparent death, functions as pure visual storytelling. No dialogue required, just a childish marker that Tangerine recognizes as a lethal warning. The film communicates through objects because its human characters are too busy performing to be legible.

The water bottle is also the film's karmic engine. Sleeping powder is introduced without ceremony. Ladybug uses it on Lemon. Lemon drinks from his water bottle on screen while you, knowing what is in it, watch in tension. When Lemon appears dead, the chain seems complete. It is not. That false death causes Tangerine's real death. Tangerine's death leaves Prince temporarily protected. Prince's protection ends when Lemon drives a truck of tangerines into her. One water bottle. Five consequences. The object persists. The humans do not.

## Choreography as Consumer-Space Combat

The dining car fight is the film's choreographic thesis. Leitch comes from John Wick, where fights unfold in wide, continuous spaces. Here, the train car is too narrow for lateral movement, so the choreography becomes vertical and rotational: bodies thrown over seats, slammed into windows, forced through doorways. The camera cannot pull back, so it stays at table height, and the fighters use trays, chairs, and food because the consumer space offers nothing else. This is violence determined by the architecture of service. The train is a series of commercial spaces (dining, quiet, bathroom), and the film treats every object within them as potential arsenal. Hiroyuki Sanada's swordfight stands out precisely because a blade requires clearance the train refuses. It is the one moment a character successfully imports a weapon from outside the system.

## The Quiet Car: Architecture as Comedy

The quiet car scene is the film's most precise architectural gag. A passenger shooshes armed killers during a fight. The comedy works because the train's social contract (politeness, enforced silence, communal proximity) momentarily overpowers the narrative contract of assassination. It is the only moment the train wins. Elsewhere, the cars are color-coded distractions (the "Tamegotchi nightmare" car, neon-soaked corridors), but the quiet car is the only one with rules that characters must obey. The humor is built on mismatches between persona and environment: Ladybug's self-help aphorisms crash against physical necessity; Lemon's Thomas the Tank Engine philosophy reduces contract killing to preschool morality; and the quiet car reduces killers to embarrassed commuters.

## The Cameo as Prop Comedy

Channing Tatum's scene is repeatedly singled out as "absolutely gold," and Ryan Reynolds appears in a voice/face role that functions as a structural interruption. These are not narrative necessities. They are prop comedy. The film changes channels on itself, importing outside star power because the train channel is getting repetitive. The cameos work like the flashbacks: as lateral intrusions that break the linear trap.

## The Tangerine Truck: Visual Pun as Catharsis

The finale resolves not through the train's logic but through its destruction. When Lemon runs over The Prince with a truck painted the same color as his code name, the catharsis is purely visual and linguistic: a fruit-colored vehicle resolving a fruit-named character. The train has been too small for this conflict. The resolution requires importing a vehicle from outside the system. It is a punchline delivered in metal and motion.

The film's karmic thesis, "You put peace out into the world, you get peace back," is engineered, not spoken. Every character who put violence into the world gets violence back. Ladybug, who put peace in, gets peace back. Prince, whose gun in her purse tipped Lemon off to her identity, is undone by her own weapon. The catharsis is the satisfaction of a closed system reaching equilibrium, but the equilibrium is achieved outside the train, in a truck, because the train was never big enough for the resolution it needed.

## Visual Grammar

- The train is a closed system: the camera cannot escape the carriages any more than the characters can. The constraint produced the film's spatial grammar.
- The grammar is lateral and interruptive: it fights the train's forward motion with constant sideways cuts, to other cars, to flashbacks, to cameos.
- The dining car fight stays at table height because the camera cannot pull back. The choreography becomes vertical and rotational because the train car is too narrow for lateral movement.
- The single master station set, redressed for every stop, creates a visual rhythm of repetition-with-variation that mirrors the foreshadowing structure: the same space returns with different details, just as the same props return with different consequences.
- The Bolivia flashback is shot as a five-second comedic cutaway, designed to read as disposable. The brevity is the disguise.
- Hiroyuki Sanada's swordfight stands out because a blade requires clearance the train refuses. It is the one moment a character successfully imports a weapon from outside the system.

## Editing

- The foreshadowing chain is an editing structure: plant (throwaway insertion) then pattern (recurrence with escalation) then payoff (consequence that retroactively rewrites the earlier scenes).
- The water bottle chain spans five edits across the film: sleeping powder introduced, Ladybug poisons the bottle, Lemon drinks (tension), false death, Tangerine's real death, Lemon survives, Lemon kills Prince. One object, five cuts, each one escalating.
- The Thomas labels recur as comic relief but each recurrence adds information until the labels become prophecy. Lemon calls The Son "Percy" (also a nod to actor Logan Lerman's Percy Jackson role). He identifies Prince as Devious Diesel long before anyone else does.
- The flashbacks are edited as speed-bumps: rapid-fire, hyper-saturated, visually louder than the present tense, but over before the emotion can land. Their velocity makes them absurd.
- The cameos function as lateral intrusions that break the linear trap, commercial breaks inside a container thriller.

## Sound Design

The film's sound design follows the grammar of interruption. The score (by Dominic Lewis) shifts genres with the flashbacks: operatic for The Wolf's wedding massacre, spaghetti western for the Bolivia job, electronic for the present-tense action. The sound effects are deliberately exaggerated: every punch, every bottle knock, every tray slap is mixed to register as comedy rather than violence. The quiet car scene is the film's most precise sonic gag: the fight continues, but the sound drops to a whisper, and the gap between the physical violence and the sonic restraint is the joke.

## Mise-en-Scène Juxtaposition

The strongest juxtaposition is the train as controlled environment vs. the chaos unfolding inside it. The Shinkansen is Japan's most regimented space: precise departure times, assigned seating, quiet cars. The film fills it with assassins, explosions, poisonings, and a truck full of tangerines. The collision between the train's institutional order and the characters' karmic chaos is the film's spatial argument: you cannot escape consequence by moving fast. The quiet car is the only space where the train's social contract momentarily overpowers the narrative contract of assassination. Everywhere else, the architecture loses.

## Production Design: Faking a Real Place

The film is named after and set entirely aboard a Tokaido Shinkansen hurtling from Tokyo to Kyoto, but main unit photography never went to Japan. From November 2020 through March 2021, the production shot on a soundstage in Los Angeles, where production designers and their crews built three physical train cars from the ground up: a first-class carriage, a lounge car, and an economy car. A driver car was built once, and its nose section doubled as both the front and the rear of the train. A single master train-station set, three to four car-lengths long, was then redressed by the art department with different floor vinyls, textures, and props to play every station the train passes through in the story. The constraint produced the film's grammar: the train is a closed system, and the camera cannot escape it any more than the characters can.

## Comedy

The comedy is structural, not tonal. The film uses comedic cutaways as foreshadowing delivery systems: the Bolivia job reads as a gag but is a setup; the Thomas labels read as comic relief but are prophecy; the water bottle reads as a throwaway prop but is a five-step chain. The humor is the mechanism by which you are taught the rules without knowing you are being taught. When the payoffs arrive, they are funny and devastating simultaneously, because the comedy was always the setup.

The humor is built on mismatches between persona and environment: Ladybug's self-help aphorisms crash against physical necessity; Lemon's Thomas the Tank Engine philosophy reduces contract killing to preschool morality; the quiet car reduces killers to embarrassed commuters. The gap between the simplicity of the instruction ("Just get off at the next stop") and the impossibility of the execution is the film's comic engine.

## Catharsis

The catharsis is karmic resolution achieved through visual pun. Every character who put violence into the world gets violence back. Ladybug, who put peace in, gets peace back. But the resolution requires the train's destruction: Lemon runs over Prince with a truck painted the same color as his code name. The train has been too small for this conflict. The resolution requires importing a vehicle from outside the system. The catharsis is a punchline delivered in metal and motion.

## Tokyo Locations

- The film is set aboard a Tokaido Shinkansen traveling from Tokyo to Kyoto, but was shot entirely on a soundstage in Los Angeles. No real Tokyo locations were used for main unit photography.
- The train itself is a fictional construction: three physical cars built from scratch, one master station set redressed for every stop.
- For the course, the film serves as a production design case study (how to fake a real place) rather than a pilgrimage location film.

## Characters

- **Ladybug**: An assassin who refuses a gun and insists he is a "bad luck charm." He survives because he puts peace into the world. His self-help aphorisms crash against physical necessity. His "bad luck" identity is self-serving narrative maintenance. He is alive while everyone else is dead. The Elder reframes his bad luck as a virtue: holding all the bad luck so others may live in peace.
- **Lemon**: One half of the twin assassin pair with Tangerine. His Thomas the Tank Engine labels are the film's forecasting system disguised as a running joke. He identifies Prince as Devious Diesel before anyone else. He survives the water bottle poisoning (bullet-proof vest) and kills Prince with a truck of tangerines, a fruit-colored vehicle resolving a fruit-named character.
- **Tangerine**: The other half. Sophisticated, adaptable. His death is caused by Lemon's false death from the water bottle chain. He refuses to leave without Lemon and the case: professionalism and loyalty become the trap.
- **Prince**: A young woman whose "innocent girl facade" is maintained by manipulation. Her gun in her purse tips Lemon off to her identity. She is undone by her own weapon and game, run over by a truck of tangerines.
- **The Elder (Kimura's father)**: The only character who manipulates for meaning rather than survival. He reframes Ladybug's bad luck as a virtue. His spinning ("they need each other; fight together or die alone") is the film's only sincere persuasion.
- **White Death**: The antagonist who orchestrates the entire train scenario. He would not have minded his son dead, meaning simply delivering the case would have worked, but the characters could not know that.
- **The Wolf (Bad Bunny)**: Granted an epic wedding massacre flashback at the speed of a trailer, then dies in thirty seconds. The film's central formal joke in miniature: epic backstory as disposable luggage.
- **The Father (Andrew Koji)**: His son pushed from a roof, his quest delivered in quick-cut exposition. Identical treatment to The Wolf: tragedy at the speed of a trailer.

## My Understanding

Bullet Train is a film about karma and persona, and its container-thriller grammar is the proof. Leitch takes the Shinkansen, Japan's most regimented space, and fills it with assassins who cannot stop performing. The train is a karmic accelerator: a closed system where no action escapes consequence. Every throwaway detail is a seed. Every comedic cutaway is a setup. The water bottle survives because it has no persona to lose. The humans keep dying because they cannot stop performing, and their performances are what kill them. The film's grammar of interruption (flashbacks, cameos, lateral cuts) fights the train's forward motion, but the train is still going forward. The catharsis is a visual pun: a truck of tangerines resolving a fruit-named character, delivered in metal and motion outside the train because the train was never big enough for the resolution it needed. The lesson for you as a filmmaker is that a container thriller is a karmic structure. You cannot leave without settling your account. (And if you think about it, that is also the business model of cinema: you sit in a sealed room, you watch people settle their accounts, and you leave when the karmic ledger is balanced.)
