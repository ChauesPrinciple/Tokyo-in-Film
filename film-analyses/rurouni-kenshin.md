# Rurouni Kenshin: The Final (2021) & The Beginning (2021), Keishi Ohtomo

## Franchise Core Thesis

The live-action Rurouni Kenshin films believe that redemption can be achieved through physical grace. The camera's continuous motion, the wire-assisted poetry of the fights, and the masochistic inward-turning violence all suggest that if a man moves beautifully enough, he can out-choreograph his own guilt. But the hot-air balloon shot in The Final knows better. It pulls back to reveal that all this elegant motion is just a man running in place while history watches from above.

To understand what these films are doing, you need to know the chanbara (samurai sword-fight) tradition they inherit. The lineage runs from Akira Kurosawa's Seven Samurai (1954) and Yojimbo (1961), through the Zatoichi series (1962-1989), to the anime OVA Trust and Betrayal (1999) that these live-action films adapt. Kurosawa shot chanbara in long takes with real spatial logic: mud, rain, exhaustion, blades that miss. The Zatoichi series made the blind swordsman's kills fast and surgical. The OVA made violence lyrical and tragic, painting sword strokes like calligraphy. Ohtomo's contribution is to synthesize all three: the spatial logic of Kurosawa, the surgical speed of Zatoichi, and the lyrical tragedy of the OVA. Kenji Tanigaki's choreography uses wires to extend the flow of action to every part of the hero's body, but the restraint is surgical: never enough to break suspension of disbelief. The result is action that feels superhuman yet self-punishing.

# The Final (2021)

## Core Thesis

The Final understands that Kenshin's reverse-blade sword is not a symbol of mercy but a trap of style. The sharp edge faces inward, yes, but the blunt edge still breaks bone. The film's camera mirrors this: it makes violence so fluid, so beautiful, so continuous that you experience it as redemption rather than repetition. Enishi's victory is that he forces Kenshin to keep performing the same motion that killed Tomoe, only now it is lit better, cut faster, and scored more elegantly. The hot-air balloon shot is the film's only honest moment because it is the only perspective from which Kenshin's grace looks like what it is: another pattern in the fire. The true subject of the film is not atonement but the aestheticization of guilt, the belief that if you kill beautifully enough, the killing becomes something else. The Final proves otherwise.

## Visual Grammar

The film is built on a bifurcated grammar that mirrors Kenshin's trap: it alternates between kinetic bodily motion where the camera is locked to the fighter's momentum, and static facial intimacy where characters reckon with the cost of that motion. Ohtomo and cinematographer Takuro Ishizaka understand that Kenshin's pacifism is a luxury of choreography: he has not stopped killing. He has only made killing beautiful. The tragedy of the film is that this beauty is so seductive you nearly forget to mourn.

### The Sakabato as Cinematic Principle

The reverse-blade sword is not merely a narrative metaphor. It is the film's visual thesis made steel. The sharp edge faces inward, toward the wielder, and the camera repeatedly returns to this detail in close-up. The action grammar that follows is masochistic: even when Kenshin dominates a fight, the camera emphasizes his own bodily vulnerability. The result is action that feels superhuman yet self-punishing: violence that always threatens to cut the one who wields it.

## Key Scenes

### The Opening: Enishi's Economy of Motion

The film begins in 1879 with police officers tracking Enishi through Tokyo streets. He subdues them with effortless force: no wasted movement, no flourish. Where Kenshin's fights will flow like water, Enishi operates with compressed, malignant precision. The camera does not need to track wildly or cut rapidly because Enishi does not expend energy he does not need. This establishes the film's negative image: a violence without grace, which makes Kenshin's graceful violence suddenly look like a style choice rather than a moral one.

### The Night Fight Before the 30-Minute Mark

This is the franchise's technical vocabulary at full concentration. Tanigaki's choreography uses wires to extend the flow of action to every part of the hero's body, but the restraint is surgical: never enough to break suspension of disbelief. The sequence is shot at night with optimal use of destructible sets, and the lighting is functional rather than atmospheric. It isolates unusual weapons and body movements against darkness, turning the brawl into a demonstration where every blade angle is legible. The camera navigates the collapsing architecture alongside the fighters. It does not cut away from impacts but moves through them. This is the film's dominant mode: editing as combat, where the frame ducks, weaves, and recovers in rhythm with the choreography. The destruction of the set is not a digital effect but a physical consequence of the fighter's own momentum.

This is the calligraphic chanbara tradition updated: the camera is the brush, the fighter's body is the stroke.

### The Intimate Scenes: Eye-Level Stasis

Between these kinetic explosions, the film spends a great deal of time on characters observing each other. Kenshin and Kaoru, Enishi and his memory of Tomoe: these moments are shot close and still, with emotion conveyed through the eyes. The camera abandons the fluid tracking of the fights for a fixed, patient gaze. This is not relief. It is accusation. The stillness forces you to look at faces that have been shaped by the same violence the action sequences celebrate. The grammar says: here is what that motion cost.

### The Hot-Air Balloons Over Burning Tokyo

The film's most devastating image is an aerial tableau: old-world war machines drifting above a modernizing capital as its streets burn. This is the only moment the camera achieves true detachment, and it exposes the lie of the kinetic grammar. For most of the runtime, you are locked to Kenshin's body, riding his speed, feeling his physical grace. But from above, that grace is indistinguishable from the fire below. Both are just patterns of destruction. The balloon's god's-eye view reveals that Kenshin's individual atonement is a footnote to historical violence: the past ascending to watch the present repeat itself. The flames are the return of Kenshin's repressed past, and the camera's slow, floating detachment makes the destruction feel inevitable rather than chaotic.

### The Halfway Confrontation: "Live in the New Age"

Around the midpoint, a defeated assassin pleads for death: "To restore the Emperor, you stole our samurai pride. You denied us an honorable death. So now... with those hands... give me that now!" The camera holds static for Kenshin's reply: "Live in the new age." This exchange embodies the film's core tension. The shot does not move. The editing does not accelerate. The words arrive with the weight of a verdict. It is the moment the film stops being an action movie and becomes a trial, with Kenshin as both defendant and unwilling executioner.

### The Final Duel

The climactic fight between Kenshin and Enishi is expected, but it hits the mark kinetically as well as emotionally. By this point, the two grammars have collapsed into each other. The motion is still fluid, the camera still locked to momentum, but every strike carries the emotional charge of the static scenes. Enishi does not want to win. He wants Kenshin to keep swinging, to prove that the reverse-blade sword is still a weapon, that atonement is just a grip technique.

## Editing

The franchise's dominant technique: cutting for kinetic continuity, not coverage. The shot flows from footwork to blade to the reaction of the environment, creating a visual syntax where the fighter and the camera share a single nervous system. When the blade is broken in Kyoto Inferno, the visual grammar fractures with it: the choreography becomes more desperate, the camera less fluid, until the weapon and the man are restored in parallel.

## Sound Design

Naoki Sato's score is distinctive but used as emotional scaffolding that the images sometimes cannot support on their own. The music does the heavy lifting in scenes where the camera cannot find the right angle for the feeling. (This will become a bigger problem in The Beginning.)

# The Beginning (2021)

## Core Thesis

The Beginning is a film that visually understands Kenshin's tragedy but narratively fears it. Its best moments are wordless: the blood soaking into snow, the countryside holding its breath, the kimonos shifting like seasons. But Ohtomo buries these images under exposition, flashbacks, and a climax that mistakes speech for drama. The film knows that the cross-shaped scar should be the endpoint of a visual journey through violence and landscape, but it keeps making characters explain the journey instead of letting you walk it. The result is a prequel that feels like a summary: all the events that matter, none of the camera movements that would make them hurt.

## Visual Grammar

The Beginning departs from Tokyo entirely, and the shift is deliberate. Where The Final burns through Tokyo with kinetic urgency, the prequel bleeds into Japanese natural landscapes with a static, picturesque patience. The camera lingers on these vistas with a patience the narrative does not earn, creating a tension between the beauty of the image and the suffering it frames.

### Blood as Environment

The film begins with its only truly free action sequences. Takeru Satoh's Kenshin is not yet the restrained wanderer. He is the Hitokiri Battosai, and the choreography here is a deliberate departure from the bloodless elegance of the previous films. The blood splatter is designed to meld with the environments: red sprayed against white snow, green foliage, brown earth. This is the film's most honest visual principle: violence not as dance but as pollution. The camera does not track with the fluid, calligraphic momentum of The Final. It holds on impacts, letting the blood settle into the natural world. The grammar says that killing leaves a stain that outlasts the body.

### The Countryside: Stasis as Tragedy

Then the film retreats into the countryside, and the grammar shifts to a patient, almost Ozu-like stillness. Ohtomo lets wide shots of mountains, fields, and falling snow do what his dialogue cannot. These are the film's true emotional anchors: wordless compositions where the landscape itself seems to mourn. (If the entire film had trusted this grammar, it would have been the best in the franchise. It does not.)

### The Robes Shot: OVA Homage as Romantic Stillness

There is a specific shot, an homage to the OVA, of Tomoe and Kenshin in robes together. It is a static, two-shot composition that relies on costume texture and body proximity rather than performance chemistry. The image is beautiful but hollow because the film has not built the kinetic or emotional bridge between these two bodies. They look like figures in a woodblock print: arranged, colored, and motionless. The shot exposes the film's central failure: it knows how to place bodies in frame but not how to make them need each other.

### Tomoe's Kimonos: Costume as Calendar

Where the film succeeds visually is in using Tomoe's constantly evolving kimonos as a timeline. Each pattern change marks a shift in her emotional state and her relationship to Kenshin without requiring dialogue. This is the grammar Ohtomo should have trusted throughout: visual chronology through textile. The camera's attention to the costumes is more specific than its attention to the actors' faces. It suggests a film that might have worked better as silent cinema, letting cloth and landscape tell the story that exposition keeps interrupting.

### The Hunched Shoulder: Satoh's Reduced Physicality

Takeru Satoh is forced into a posture that reviewers describe as moody emo: hunching his shoulders, withdrawing his physicality. This is a catastrophic misuse of the franchise's greatest asset. In the previous films, Satoh's body was the camera's dance partner. Here, it is closed off, folded inward. The grammar of the action films required openness, extension, flow. The grammar of this romance requires the opposite, but Ohtomo does not find a cinematic language for interiority. He simply has Satoh shrink. The result is that Kenshin's transformation from killer to lover is told through posture rather than shot composition, and the camera seems embarrassed by its own protagonist.

## Key Scenes

### The Climax: Speech Replacing Steel

The climactic battle is the film's formal collapse. A random antagonist appears and delivers a long monologue justifying his actions before a fight that is more dramatic than choreography-based. This is the franchise's kinetic grammar abandoned entirely. The camera, which in The Final moved through collapsing architecture in continuous motion, here sits statically while men talk about their feelings, then stages a duel with no stakes because the opponent has no visual or emotional relationship to Kenshin. It is anti-cinema: the word made flesh, and the flesh made boring.

## Sound Design

Naoki Sato's score is much more distinctive and unique for this film than the others, and far too many emotional scenes only carry weight because of the excellent score. The film's grammar is so uncertain that it outsources its emotional architecture to the music. When the images fail and the dialogue clunks, the strings swell to remind you that you are supposed to feel something. It is a confession of directorial failure: Ohtomo does not know where to put the camera to make you cry, so he lets the composer do it.

## My Understanding

The Rurouni Kenshin franchise is a study in what happens when action grammar becomes so beautiful it functions as moral evasion. The Final understands this and makes it the thesis: Kenshin's grace is a trap, and the hot-air balloon shot exposes it. The Beginning does not understand its own material: it has the visual vocabulary (blood as environment, landscape as mourning, kimonos as timeline) but keeps interrupting it with speech. The franchise's greatest asset is Kenji Tanigaki's choreography and Takeru Satoh's physicality. When the camera trusts the body, the films are extraordinary. When it trusts the word, they collapse. The lesson for you as a filmmaker is simple: if your film is about a man who cannot speak his guilt, do not make him speak it. Make him move.
