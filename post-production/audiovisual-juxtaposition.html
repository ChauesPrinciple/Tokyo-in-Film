Audiovisual Juxtaposition

Purpose

This assignment invites you to analyze how editors and sound designers pair contrasting sound with image to create irony, tension, or psychological depth. When the soundtrack collides with the image, the audience is forced to reconcile the difference and find a third, deeper meaning.

The Concept

Sound in cinema is an active storytelling partner. Key techniques of audiovisual contrast include:

  • Contrapuntal Music: Placing upbeat, cheerful, or classical music over scenes of violence, panic, or melancholy.
  • Dialogue vs. Visual Reality: An interview or monologue claiming one reality while on-screen action contradicts it.
  • Acoustic Scale Contrast: Cutting abruptly from overwhelming urban noise to dead silence, or using a quiet sound to build unbearable tension.

Films to Choose From

Analyze audiovisual juxtaposition in a scene from one of these films:

  • Chainsaw Man: Reze Arc, Tatsuya Yoshihara (2025)
  • Your Name, Makoto Shinkai (2016)
  • Jiro Dreams of Sushi, David Gelb (2011)
  • Rurouni Kenshin: Final Chapter Part II - The Beginning, Keishi Otomo (2021)

Prompts

Prompt 1: The Sonic Collision

Goal: Select a scene and timestamp where audio runs contrary to what is on screen. Describe both tracks.

Ask Yourself:

  • What is happening visually at this moment?
  • What is happening sonically? Is it contrapuntal music, contradictory dialogue, or an acoustic scale shift?
  • How would the scene feel if the sound matched the image? What does the mismatch do?
Prompt 2: The Third Meaning

Goal: Explain what new irony, subtext, or tension the collision between audio and image creates.

Ask Yourself:

  • What is the image saying? What is the sound saying? What is the third thing they say together?
  • Does the contrast create irony, empathy, dread, or something else?
  • Is the filmmaker commenting on the action, deepening it, or undercutting it?
Prompt 3: Post-Production Application

Goal: Translate the technique into your own Tokyo-based post-production plan.

Ask Yourself:

  • How can you pair recorded Tokyo environmental sound or music against contrasting footage to elevate subtext?
  • What happens if you lay a gentle melody over chaotic Shibuya crossing footage?
  • What happens if you drop all sound during a moment of high visual energy?

Textbook Contribution

If your analysis reveals how the audiovisual collision generates a third meaning, it belongs in the book.

  • The Collision: What two tracks were placed in friction?
  • The Third Meaning: What did the audience generate from the contrast?
  • The Application: How can the technique be replicated in Tokyo post-production?

Deliverables

  • Length: 500+ words. Journals are short, which fools people into thinking they are casual. They are not. The compression is the difficulty, so every sentence has to earn its place.
  • Film: one approved film from the list above. An unapproved film is a zero, no matter how good the writing is.
  • Sources: none required. The film itself is your evidence.
  • Citations: an exact MLA timestamp on every claim, plus a Works Cited entry for the film. A timestamp works like a page number in an essay about a novel. It tells your reader exactly where to look so they can check your claim against the film themselves. If the sound and the image it contradicts happen at different moments, write a timestamp for each of them.
  • Vocabulary: the terminology of this module (contrapuntal music, diegetic vs. non-diegetic, acoustic scale contrast, audiovisual juxtaposition, subtext, the third meaning).

How to Write This Journal

The Core Rule

Every claim does three things at once:

  • Names the technique in this module's vocabulary: contrapuntal music, diegetic vs. non-diegetic, acoustic scale contrast, audiovisual juxtaposition, the third meaning.
  • Locates it at an exact second in MLA form: (00:14:46).
  • Explains what it does to the meaning of the film, or to the viewer.

Use each term precisely rather than as a general impression of how the scene felt. Check every timestamp in the film instead of estimating it. When you explain what a technique does, do more than name it, because naming a technique is not the same as explaining its effect. After each piece of evidence, ask yourself so what?

MLA Timestamps

A timestamp gives the hour, the minute, and the second, each field padded with a leading zero, separated by colons, and enclosed in parentheses: (00:14:46). You cite one second rather than a range.

Incorrect Correct Why
(00:14:00 to 00:15:30)(00:14:46)A ninety second range does not tell your reader which moment you mean, so give the exact second where it happens.
at (00:14:46)(00:14:46)The parentheses already tell your reader this is a location in the film, so the word "at" adds nothing.
(14:46)(00:14:46)The hour field is always included, even when the moment falls in the first hour of the film.
(1:31:30)(01:31:30)Every field takes two digits, so an hour written as a single digit needs a leading zero.
(00:01:37:04)(00:01:37)A fourth number counts frames, which belongs in your editing software rather than in an essay.
(00:15:00) for a moment at (00:14:46)(00:14:46)Rounding to the nearest minute sends your reader to a moment where nothing you described happens.
around (00:48:00)(00:48:12)An approximate timestamp cannot be checked, so go back to the film and find the exact second.

Accuracy

If you write "contrapuntal music (00:14:46)" and that second has no music, or the music matches the mood of the image, the claim fails. Go to the second, listen to the soundtrack, look at the screen, and name what is there. Describe what is actually in the audio and what is actually in the frame.

Minimums

  • Form, not plot. "The character looks sad while happy music plays" is plot. "The contrapuntal cue (00:22:04) lays a major-key melody over the character's breakdown, so the music does not comfort her; it mocks her" is form.
  • Claims, not chronology. Group by idea, not first scene to last.
  • A Works Cited entry in MLA 9: Title of the Film. Directed by Firstname Lastname, Production Company, Year.

Evaluation Criteria

  • Content — 25. Accurate terms, accurate claims, depth past description.
  • Structure — 25. The introduction narrows to a thesis; the analysis proves it and closes.
  • Voice and Mechanics — 25. Grammar, register, sentence control, correct header.
  • Documentation — 5. Timestamp format, Works Cited, italicized titles.

A malformed timestamp costs Documentation. A missing one costs Content, which is worth five times as much.
