3.5

The Rhythm of Process

Purpose

This assignment invites you to analyze how editors use montage and rhythm to depict the mastery of a skill. Using Jiro Dreams of Sushi (2011) as our reference example, you will examine how time compression and repetitive cutting patterns allow the audience to "learn" the process alongside the master.

Jiro Dreams of Sushi (2011), David Gelb

The Rhythm

Process is boring. Watching a man massage an octopus for 45 minutes is not cinematic. But watching three seconds of massaging, cut to three seconds of slicing, cut to three seconds of pressing? That is a rhythm. In Jiro Dreams of Sushi, the editing mimics the cadence of Jiro's routine.

The film uses a specific editing pattern to demonstrate Jiro's mastery: Repetition. We see the same shot of the fish market, the same shot of the rice, the same shot of the plate, over and over. But each time, the pace quickens or the focus tightens. The editor, Brandon Driscoll-Luttringer, aligns the cuts with the classical music score, turning the act of cooking into a rhythmic sequence.

The "Mastery" is not just in Jiro's hands; it is in the timeline. By removing the mistakes, the pauses, and the waiting, the editor constructs a version of reality where perfection is continuous. The montage manipulates time to convince us that Jiro is the process.

How it Works

  • The Routine: Establishing a baseline rhythm of the daily routine (Train, Market, Kitchen).
  • The Compression (The Montage): Compressing hours of labor (massaging the octopus) into a flurry of cuts to show effort without boredom.
  • The Mastery: The final sequence of serving sushi, where the cuts are seamless, matching the fluid motion of the master.

Films to Choose From

Analyze the rhythm of process in a scene from one of these films:

  • Fast and Furious: Tokyo Drift, Justin Lin (The drift training montage)
  • Initial D, Andrew Lau (The cup of water drift technique)
  • Exit 8, Genki Kawamura (The corridor loop and anomaly scan)
  • Ramen Heads, Koki Shigeno (The soup preparation)

Prompts

Prompt 1: The Steps

Goal: Deconstruct the mechanics of the skill.

Ask Yourself:

  • What specific steps does the editor choose to show (e.g., Shifting gears, slicing fish)?
  • What steps are skipped? Why?
  • How does the sound design emphasize the mechanical nature of the act?

Example: In Jiro, we see the knife slice, but we rarely see the cleaning of the knife. The editor focuses on the action, not the maintenance.

Prompt 2: The Compression (The Montage)

Goal: Analyze how time is manipulated.

Ask Yourself:

  • Does the scene feel faster or slower than real time?
  • How does the music drive the pace of the cuts?
  • Does the rhythm accelerate as the character gets better at the skill?

Example: In Tokyo Drift, the first drift attempts are choppy and chaotic. As Sean learns, the shots become longer and more fluid. The editing mirrors his competence.

Prompt 3: The Mastery

Goal: Explain how the edit sells the expertise.

Ask Yourself:

  • How does the final sequence prove the character is a master?
  • Is there a "flow state" where the editing becomes invisible?
  • Does the editor use slow motion to emphasize a specific detail of the technique?

Example: The final service in Jiro is presented almost without dialogue. The rhythm of the sushi being placed on the plate tells the whole story.

Textbook Contribution

If your analysis reveals the rhythm of the scene, it belongs in the book.

  • The Technique: What skill was analyzed?
  • The Edit: How was it constructed?
  • The Takeaway: What does the rhythm tell us about the character?

Deliverables

  • Length: 500+ words. Journals are short, which fools people into thinking they are casual. They are not. The compression is the difficulty, so every sentence has to earn its place.
  • Film: one approved film from the list above. An unapproved film is a zero, no matter how good the writing is.
  • Sources: none required. The film itself is your evidence.
  • Citations: exact MLA timestamps on every claim, plus a Works Cited entry for the film.
  • Vocabulary: the terminology of this module (montage, rhythm, average shot length, compression, ellipsis).

How to Write This Journal

The Core Rule

Every claim does three things at once:

  • Names the technique in this module's vocabulary: montage, rhythm, average shot length, compression, ellipsis.
  • Locates it at an exact second in MLA form: (00:14:46).
  • Explains what it does to the meaning of the film, or to the viewer.

No term is an impression. No timestamp is a guess. No explanation is a label. After each piece of evidence, ask so what?

MLA Timestamps

A timestamp gives the hour, the minute, and the second, each field padded with a leading zero, separated by colons, and enclosed in parentheses: (00:14:46). You cite one second rather than a range.

Incorrect Correct Why
(00:14:00 to 00:15:30)(00:14:46)That is a range. Your claim happens at one second, so cite that second.
at (00:14:46)(00:14:46)The parenthetical is the citation. The preposition is not part of it.
(14:46)(00:14:46)The hour field is required, even when it is zero.
(1:31:30)(01:31:30)Each field is two digits.
(00:01:37:04)(00:01:37)A fourth field counts frames. That is timecode from an edit suite, not a citation.
(00:15:00) for a moment at (00:14:46)(00:14:46)Rounding to the minute sends your reader to a second where your evidence is not.
around (00:48:00)(00:48:12)A hedge is not a citation. Give the second it happens.

Accuracy

If you write "match cut (00:14:46)" and that second is a dissolve, the claim fails. Go to the second, look at the screen, and name what is there. Describe the outgoing shot, the incoming shot, and where the cut falls against the sound.

Minimums

  • An approved film. Anything else is a zero.
  • This module's terminology. An editing journal analyzes editing.
  • An exact timestamp on every claim. Comparing an early passage to a late one takes two citations.
  • Form, not plot. "The protagonist runs through the rain" is plot. "The tracking shot (00:22:04) holds the same distance from him for the entire run, so we are not watching him flee; we are fleeing with him" is form.
  • Claims, not chronology. Group by idea, not first scene to last.
  • A Works Cited entry in MLA 9: Title of the Film. Directed by Firstname Lastname, Production Company, Year.

Evaluation Criteria

  • Content — 25. Accurate terms, accurate claims, depth past description.
  • Structure — 25. The introduction narrows to a thesis; the analysis proves it and closes.
  • Voice and Mechanics — 25. Grammar, register, sentence control, correct header.
  • Documentation — 5. Timestamp format, Works Cited, italicized titles.

A malformed timestamp costs Documentation. A missing one costs Content, which is worth five times as much.
