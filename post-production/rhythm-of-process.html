3.5

The Rhythm of Process

Purpose

This assignment invites you to analyze how editors use montage and rhythm to depict the mastery of a skill. Using Jiro Dreams of Sushi (2011) as our reference example, you will examine how time compression and repetitive cutting patterns allow the audience to "learn" the process alongside the master.

Jiro Dreams of Sushi (2011), David Gelb

The Pulse

Process is boring. Watching a man massage an octopus for 45 minutes is not cinematic. But watching three seconds of massaging, cut to three seconds of slicing, cut to three seconds of pressing? That is a rhythm. That is a pulse. In Jiro Dreams of Sushi, the editing mimics the heartbeat of Jiro's routine.

The film uses a specific editing pattern to demonstrate Jiro's mastery: Repetition. We see the same shot of the fish market, the same shot of the rice, the same shot of the plate, over and over. But each time, the pace quickens or the focus tightens. The editor, Brandon Driscoll-Luttringer, aligns the cuts with the classical music score, turning the act of cooking into a dance.

The "Mastery" is not just in Jiro's hands; it is in the timeline. By removing the mistakes, the pauses, and the waiting, the editor constructs a version of reality where perfection is continuous. The montage manipulates time to convince us that Jiro is the process.

How it Works

  • The Ritual (The Beat): Establishing a baseline rhythm of the daily routine (Train, Market, Kitchen).
  • The Compression (The Montage): Compressing hours of labor (massaging the octopus) into a flurry of cuts to show effort without boredom.
  • The Flow (The Mastery): The final sequence of serving sushi, where the cuts are seamless, matching the fluid motion of the master.

Films to Choose From

Analyze the rhythm of process in a scene from one of these films:

  • Fast and Furious: Tokyo Drift, Justin Lin (The drift training montage)
  • Initial D, Andrew Lau (The cup of water drift technique)
  • Exit 8, Genki Kawamura (The corridor loop and anomaly scan)
  • Ramen Heads, Koki Shigeno (The soup preparation)

Prompts

Prompt 1: The Steps (The Ritual)

Goal: Deconstruct the mechanics of the skill.

Ask Yourself:

  • What specific steps does the editor choose to show (e.g., Shifting gears, slicing fish)?
  • What steps are skipped? Why?
  • How does the sound design emphasize the mechanical nature of the act?

Example: In Jiro, we see the knife slice, but we rarely see the cleaning of the knife. The editor focuses on the action, not the maintenance.

Prompt 2: The Compression (The Montage)

Goal: Analyze how time is manipulated.

Ask Yourself:

  • Does the scene feel faster or slower than real time?
  • How does the music drive the pace of the cuts?
  • Does the rhythm accelerate as the character gets better at the skill?

Example: In Tokyo Drift, the first drift attempts are choppy and chaotic. As Sean learns, the shots become longer and more fluid. The editing mirrors his competence.

Prompt 3: The Flow (The Mastery)

Goal: Explain how the edit sells the expertise.

Ask Yourself:

  • How does the final sequence prove the character is a master?
  • Is there a "flow state" where the editing becomes invisible?
  • Does the editor use slow motion to emphasize a specific detail of the technique?

Example: The final service in Jiro is presented almost without dialogue. The rhythm of the sushi being placed on the plate tells the whole story.

Textbook Contribution

If your analysis reveals the hidden rhythm of the scene, it belongs in the book.

  • The Technique: What skill was analyzed?
  • The Edit: How was it constructed?
  • The Takeaway: What does the rhythm tell us about the character?

Deliverables

  • Length: 500+ words. Journals are short, which fools people into thinking they are casual. They are not. The compression is the difficulty, so every sentence has to earn its place.
  • Film: one approved film from the list above. An unapproved film is a zero, no matter how good the writing is.
  • Sources: none required. The film itself is your evidence.
  • Citations: exact MLA timestamps on every claim, plus a Works Cited entry for the film.
  • Vocabulary: the terminology of this module (montage, rhythm, average shot length, compression, ellipsis).

How to Write This Journal

A journal is a compressed piece of formal analysis of an approved film, written in the vocabulary of the module you are currently in. Three or four claims built properly will always beat a tour of the whole film.

The Core Rule: A Film Term, an Exact Timestamp, and What It Does

Every analytical claim you make has to do three things at once.

It has to name the technique in the vocabulary of this module. Telling me the montage is fast names nothing at all, while identifying an average shot length, an ellipsis, a match cut, a cut placed on the downbeat of the score, or a shift from choppy to sustained coverage gives me a term that you can then be held to.

It has to locate that technique at an exact second of the film, cited in MLA form as (00:14:46).

Then it has to explain what the technique does, which is the move that separates analysis from description. The question is not what the technique is, nor what happens in the plot while it occurs, but what it does to the meaning of the film or to the person watching it.

A claim that names no technique remains an impression, a technique carrying no timestamp remains a guess, and a technique with a timestamp but no explanation remains a label. Only all three together amount to analysis, and analysis is what is being graded.

The test you can run on your own draft: after each piece of evidence, ask "so what?" If your paragraph has no answer, you have described the film. If it has one, you have analyzed it.

MLA Timestamps, Precisely

MLA cites time-based media by timestamp, in hours, minutes, and seconds. The citation keeps all three fields along with their leading zeros, separates them with colons, and sits in parentheses inside your sentence the way any other citation would: (00:14:46).

Read that as fourteen minutes and forty-six seconds. It points at one second of film. That is the entire purpose of the citation: I should be able to open the film, go to that second, and see the thing you say is there. Editing analysis lives or dies on this, because a cut is an event that happens at one second and nowhere else.

Common failures, and the fix for each:

  • (00:14:00 to 00:15:30) A range is a neighborhood, not an address. I cannot verify a claim against ninety seconds of film. Write (00:14:46).
  • at (00:14:46) The word "at" is not part of MLA. The parenthetical stands on its own, as in: the match cut (00:14:46) joins the two images.
  • (14:46) The hour field is missing, so the reference is under-specified. Write (00:14:46).
  • (1:31:30) The leading zero was dropped. Write (01:31:30).
  • (00:01:37:04) Four fields belong to the production timecode, where the last figure counts frames. I cannot tell which figure is your seconds count. Write (00:01:37).
  • (00:15:00) The timestamp has been rounded to the whole minute, but the cut you are describing does not fall there. Write the second it actually happens, such as (00:15:07).
  • "around the middle of the film" "Around," "about," "roughly," and "somewhere near" are not citations. Write (00:48:12).

The Timestamp Has to Match the Term, and the Term Has to Match the Screen

This is where most analysis actually breaks. If you write "match cut (00:14:46)" and that second of film is a dissolve, the sentence fails. Not because the prose is weak, but because the term and the image do not agree, and once they disagree, nothing built on top of them can stand. Go to the second. Look at what is on the screen. Name that.

The same holds for the description around the citation. Tell me what is actually visible and audible at that moment: what the outgoing shot was, what the incoming shot is, where the cut falls against the music, how long the shots are holding. Specific knowledge of the moment is the proof that you watched the film rather than remembered it.

Minimums

Every journal needs to do all of the following:

  • Use an approved film for this module. An unapproved film is a zero, no matter how good the writing is. Check the list before you start watching.
  • Use the terminology of this module. An editing journal analyzes editing. If it drifts into costume and production design for their own sake, it is answering a different assignment.
  • Anchor every claim with an exact timestamp. If you are comparing an early passage to a late one, that is two citations, not one.
  • Analyze form, not plot. "The protagonist runs through the rain" is plot. "The tracking shot (00:22:04) holds the same distance from him for the entire run, so we are not watching him flee; we are fleeing with him" is form. Form is how the film was built. That is our subject.
  • Organize around claims, not around the clock. Walking through the film from first scene to last is the most common structural failure. Group your observations by idea instead, and let the film's chronology come apart if it needs to.
  • Include a Works Cited entry for the film in MLA 9, with the title italicized: Title of the Film. Directed by Firstname Lastname, Production Company, Year.

Evaluation Criteria

  • Content (25 points): Accurate terms, accurate claims about the film, and depth past description. The term-plus-timestamp-plus-mechanism unit lives here. Naming a technique without explaining what it does costs Content points, and so does plot summary standing in for analysis.
  • Structure (25 points): Organizational logic. The introduction narrows to a thesis, and the analysis that follows proves that thesis and closes. What I am reading for is whether one idea leads to the next, and whether the journal was built rather than poured.
  • Voice and Mechanics (25 points): Grammar, register, sentence control, and a correct header. Write like someone making an argument to a reader who is skeptical.
  • Documentation (5 points): The format of your citations: timestamps shaped correctly, Works Cited present and correct, titles italicized.

Notice where the timestamps actually count. A malformed timestamp is a small Documentation problem. Missing timestamps are a Content problem, and Content is worth five times what Documentation is worth, because without them you have no evidence and therefore no analysis. Not having timestamps is not a formatting slip. It is a loss of the substance of the assignment.
