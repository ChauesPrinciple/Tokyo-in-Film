## Pre-Production Phase

### 5/13: Logline + Catharsis Pressure Map

After Quiz 1. Screen in class: the opening of Godzilla Minus One (Takashi Yamazaki, 2023), from the kamikaze base sequence through Koichi's return to Tokyo. Approximately the first 15 minutes. This is one of the four films available for the 1.6 Catharsis assignment, which means the activity doubles as live preparation for that essay.

Part 1: Logline (Script deliverable prep)

Write one sentence: A [protagonist] must [goal], but [obstacle] stands in the way, or else [stakes]. Maximum 35 words. Specific, visible actions only. Vague verbs are rejected ("discovers who they are," "goes on a journey"). The logline is the spine of the Script deliverable, so the sentence written here is the sentence the script gets built from.

Success criterion: someone who has not seen your film can read the sentence and describe the first scene.

Part 2: Catharsis Pressure Map (1.6 Catharsis prep)

The 1.6 Catharsis assignment asks for a 500-word analysis mapping a film onto three stages: the Inhale (the pressure the film makes you carry), the Withholding (what the film refuses to release, and for how long), and the Exhale (whether the ending earns its release). Approved films for the essay: 13 Assassins, Kubi, Godzilla Minus One, Chainsaw Man: Reze Arc.

Apply that framework to what you just watched, in four sentences total:

1. What is the Inhale carrying? Name the pressure source. Not "the war"; name the specific image or action.
2. What is being withheld? Reunion, safety, forgiveness, an answer. Be specific.
3. Identify one technique sustaining the withholding: composition, lighting, sound, performance.
4. What ending would feel earned? What ending would feel unearned?

Students choosing Godzilla Minus One for the 1.6 essay now have the opening scaffolded. Students choosing one of the other three films are practicing the framework on a real example before applying it to theirs.

Standard for the written response: name one compositional choice you actually observed, name what it produces emotionally, name what would change if the choice were different. "It feels sad" is not a response. The instructor will model live specificity after rewatching the opening before class. Vague answers are sent back.

Post both parts to the Stream.

### 5/14: Framing Redesign

After Quiz 2. Use a paused frame from Chainsaw Man: Reze Arc (Tatsuya Yoshihara, MAPPA, 2025), a two-character scene from the Kanda cafe sequences where Denji and Reze meet.

Draw the existing frame in your notebook. Note where the subjects are placed, what shape they form, what the lighting is doing, whether the composition is symmetrical or asymmetrical, and what the negative space communicates.

Redesign the shot using at least one of the following moves:

- Rule of thirds: move the dominant subject to a power position off-center.
- Triangle direction: stable base-down for authority, inverted for instability.
- Symmetry inversion: flip symmetrical to asymmetrical, or the reverse.
- Negative space: add empty space behind or around one character to signal isolation, surveillance, or imminent absence.

Then write two or three sentences explaining how the new composition changes what the audience reads about the power dynamic. If the lighting logic has to change with your redesign, name that too. Vague responses ("it changes the mood") are not accepted. Compare to the demo below.

Demo before students start: "Denji's placement at the center of a symmetrical frame communicates that the cafe is a safe space he controls. In my redesign I pushed him to the left edge against the negative space of the window, which makes him look like he is about to disappear from the scene Reze is constructing around him."

Final step: sketch the opening frame of one scene from your own Scene Project film. Apply at least one of the same compositional moves and explain in one sentence what you want it to communicate. This sketch goes into the Storyboard deliverable.

Post the redesigned sketch, the written response, and the Scene Project opening frame to the Stream.

## Pre-Production: Excursion Preparation

### 5/18: Pilgrimage Shots Location Plan (Reze Arc)

No quiz today. The full period is the in-class draft of the 1.1.5 Pilgrimage Shots Location Plan, focused on Chainsaw Man: Reze Arc Tokyo locations.

Part A: Location Research and Floor Plan

Each student or group selects one specific scene from Reze Arc set in a confirmable Tokyo location (Kanda cafe area, Yotsuya, Shimbashi, or other identifiable neighborhood). Confirm the real-world location now. The Pilgrimage Shots form requires a Google Maps pin before it will accept your submission.

Draw a bird's-eye floor plan showing:

- Subject positions and blocking
- Camera placement and angle
- Light sources (account for street lighting at the time of day you plan to shoot)
- How action moves through the space

Note for students: If you arrive on location and discover you cannot recreate the frame because you never confirmed the angle, you have wasted the excursion. That preparation is happening now, not on the day.

Part B: Pilgrimage Storyboard

Storyboard the shots you plan to capture at your location:

- The recreation shot matching the specific Reze Arc frame
- The establishing shot proving you are at the real location (not a similar-looking one)
- At least two support shots: geography, texture, or human rhythm at the location

For each panel: shot type, angle, subject position, any camera movement. Arrows and labeled boxes are sufficient.

Post the floor plan and storyboard to the Stream. Submit the confirmed Google Maps pin before the period ends.

### 5/19: Geometry, Simple to Complex

After Quiz 3. Two scenes, screened in order. The simple scene teaches the observational tools. The complex scene tests whether students can deploy them on geometry that does not reduce to a single line.

Stage 1: Tokyo Drift (Justin Lin, 2006), parking structure scene where DK and his crew face Sean and Han.

1. Draw the scene from above. Mark the line separating DK's side from Sean and Han's side.

2. Count three things: which side has more screen space (width and depth), which side has more bodies, which side is closer to the camera. Write one sentence naming who owns the frame in the opening shot.

3. On the second viewing, find one moment a body crosses the dividing line, or the line itself moves (camera reframe, blocking shift). Circle the timestamp on your floor plan.

4. Write two sentences: what changes at the crossing, and what the crossing communicates that no character has yet said. If no body crosses and the line never moves, write two sentences on why the geometry stays locked and what that communicates.

Stage 2: Kubi (Takeshi Kitano, 2023), opening court scene around Nobunaga.

1. Find the apex. Mark the position the other bodies orient toward: where they bow, where their gazes track, where their shoulders square.

2. Draw the shape the retainers form around the apex: triangle base-down, semicircle, two parallel rows, irregular cluster. Label the retainer who occupies the most frame space after the apex.

3. On the second viewing, find one body breaking the formation: a glance off-axis, a delayed bow, shoulders angled toward an exit, a head turn at the wrong moment. Circle the break on your floor plan and timestamp it.

4. Write three sentences: what the formation says about the political situation, what the break says that the formation alone does not, and what dialogue would have to be added to communicate the same thing the geometry already has.

Scene Project Geometry Sketch

Pick a scene from your Scene Project film. Decide first whether your geometry is binary (Stage 1 toolkit) or hierarchical (Stage 2 toolkit). Draw the geometry from above using the appropriate tools. Identify the line or apex, the real estate or formation shape, the planned crossing or break. Write one sentence on what the geometry communicates that the dialogue does not.

This sketch and sentence are the seed of the Geometry Worksheet add-on.

Post both floor plans (Tokyo Drift with crossing circled, Kubi with break circled), the two-sentence Tokyo Drift reading, the three-sentence Kubi reading, the Scene Project sketch, and the one-sentence Scene Project justification.

## Production Phase

### 5/21: Sound Layer Map

After Quiz 4. Watch three minutes of Ramen Heads (Koki Shigeno, 2017), the soup preparation sequence with Osamu Tomita. Watch once with sound, once muted.

On the muted pass, list every sound that should be present. Label each one:

- D Diegetic
- ND Non-diegetic
- F Foley (liquid, metal, steam, knife contact)
- ADR Narration or dialogue recorded after filming
- RT Room tone (the specific ambient sound of that kitchen)

Identify the three most important sounds and explain in one sentence each why they matter to the scene.

Then answer in one or two sentences: What does the sound design tell us about the chef's relationship to his process that the image alone does not?

Final step: write a sound plan for your Favorite Place location. Name the three most important sounds you expect to capture there (D, F, or RT) and write one sentence per sound on how it will communicate something about the place. This goes into the Favorite Place add-on form under the audio strategy section.

Demo before students start: "Temple bell fading into cicadas with distant train, not ambient sounds." That precision starts now, in this activity, not on the day of recording.

Show the Location Sound Log worksheet for two minutes at the end.

Post the labeled list, the three justifications, the Ramen Heads sound design response, and the Favorite Place sound plan.

## Post-Production Phase

### 5/25: Editing Rhythm + Kuleshov Juxtaposition

No quiz today. Two activities, both feeding the Post-Production add-on assignments.

Activity A: Rhythm of Process (3.5 deliverable prep)

Return to the Ramen Heads soup preparation sequence with sound on. Identify the three stages from the 3.5 assignment:

- The Ritual. What specific steps does the editor choose to show? What is skipped, and why?
- The Compression. Does the scene feel faster or slower than real time? How does the sound drive the pace of the cuts?
- The Flow. Is there a moment where the editing becomes nearly invisible, where the rhythm and the process merge?

Calculate the Average Shot Length for any 60-second stretch: total seconds divided by number of cuts.

Final step: write one sentence identifying which Rhythm of Process stage is hardest to achieve in your own Favorite Place edit, and why. That sentence is the thesis of your 3.5 Rhythm of Process written analysis.

Post the three-stage breakdown, the ASL figure, and the thesis sentence.

Activity B: Kuleshov Juxtaposition Analysis

Film: Chainsaw Man: Reze Arc — the final sequence, from the theater scene with Makima through the alleyway confrontation. By this point in the term you have worked with Reze Arc twice already, so the film is familiar and the focus stays on the editing.

Break the sequence into at least eight distinct cuts. For each cut, fill in three columns:

- What you see. Framing, subject, movement.
- What came immediately before. The previous shot's emotional tone or content.
- What the juxtaposition produces. What does placing this shot after the previous shot generate, that the shot alone would not?

Final synthesis in two or three sentences: What do these edits reveal about Denji's emotional position at the moment of the betrayal that dialogue or performance alone could not?

Note before students start: The third column is where the analysis happens. If the column just describes the cut again, you have described two shots, not juxtaposition. The question to answer: what does A before B produce that B alone would not?

A previous student model (Tulip Collier, 2025, working on a different sequence): "Tetsuo and Kaneda drawing on pavement, before: Tetsuo being handed away, meaning: moment of innocence with Kaneda vs. past neglect." That three-column structure is the standard.

Post the eight-cut breakdown and the synthesis paragraph.

### 5/26: Sound Without Words

After Quiz 5. Film: Tsukigakirei (As The Moon, So Beautiful), Episode 8, from 14:20 to 17:32. The scene has no dialogue.

Watch twice: once with sound, once muted.

Answer all four questions in one or two sentences each:

1. What story elements remain fully clear without language? What does the action alone communicate?
2. How do sound design and score communicate tenderness, distance, or urgency here?
3. What emotional information is preserved or enhanced by silence, ambient sound, or underscore alone?
4. Would the scene still land without language? Why?

Run a brief share-out on question 4. A previous student response (Victoria, 2025) sets the target: "We don't need words to feel the butterflies between them. Through action, the boy spends all of his money to make the girl happy."

Final step: identify one scene in your own Scene Project where sound will carry what dialogue cannot, or where you will use silence deliberately. One sentence describing the moment and what the silence or ambient sound will communicate. This goes into the Tone Flip planning.

Post responses to all four questions and the Scene Project sentence.

### 5/27: Rewrite the Soundscape (Tone Flip prep)

Open work period or outside class. Video posted by end of day. This activity directly produces a draft of the Tone Flip add-on deliverable.

Choose any 15-second section from any film. Create an alternative sound design:

- Replace the music with something genre-unexpected (jazz over a tense confrontation, silence over action, ambient rain over domestic calm).
- Exaggerate one Foley source (breathing, footsteps, steam, metal) while reducing everything else.

Write a justification of three or four sentences:

- What is gained by the change?
- What is lost?
- How does the new sound design change what the audience interprets about the scene?

Demo standard from prior cohorts: A 2025 student replaced the score in the Spirited Away scene where Chihiro discovers her parents have turned into pigs and made the moment genuinely horrifying. Another student took a Glee classroom scene and made the teacher feel dangerous. A third stripped everything to ambience alone. All three worked because the justification did not stop at "I added scary music." It named what changed: "the scene now communicates dread instead of wonder because the only sound the audience hears is breathing accelerating." That precision is the standard.

Post the video file and the written justification.

After Quiz 6. Screen an unseen clip of three to five minutes. Students write a 20 to 25-minute analytical response addressing all four:

- Use at least three course terms accurately and in context.
