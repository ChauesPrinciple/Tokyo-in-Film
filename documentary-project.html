Documentary Project

One place. One craft. Two cuts.
Tokyo through your own lens.

You are going to make a short documentary about something in this city you genuinely love, using the films you study this semester as your visual vocabulary. In self-selected teams of three, you will document how you see Tokyo.

This is the infinite game: there is no single winning move, no perfect documentary, only the next interesting choice. Your subject can be a place (a neighborhood, a shop, a station, a shrine, a river walk) or a craft you encountered through the films (tate sword choreography, taido, voice acting, ramen making, sushi making). Loved the food films the most? Make a ramen documentary that borrows its eyes from Ramen Heads and Tampopo. Loved the action? Document a dojo the way Rurouni Kenshin covers a fight, without throwing a single punch yourself.

The Assignment: Pick Your Subject

Choose one of two strands. Both end in the same deliverables.

1. A Place You Love

A neighborhood, shop, arcade, station, shrine, or street. Your documentary makes the case for why this place matters, told through the people, sounds, and rhythms that live there.

2. A Craft You Discovered

A skill you met through the films: tate, taido, voice acting, ramen or sushi craft, drift culture. Find its practitioners in Tokyo and document how the craft actually works.

Steal Like a Documentarian: The Shot Recreations

Here is the spine of the project. Your documentary must contain at least 7 recreated shots from the 2026 film list, re-staged at real Tokyo locations: at minimum 2 establishing shots and 2 B-roll shots (cut-ins or cutaways), with the remaining 3 drawn from either category. These are not decorations. They are working shots that carry your documentary the same way they carried their films.

Every recreation requires a Shot-Match Sheet (one page each) proving you understood the frame before you rebuilt it:

  • The original: frame grab, film title, timestamp.
  • Your recreation: the matching frame from your footage.
  • Composition analysis: rule of thirds placement, leading lines, headroom and looking room, foreground/midground/background layers, camera height, estimated focal length, and light direction or time of day.
  • One sentence each: what the original shot is doing for its film, and what your recreation does for yours.

Where to Steal From

  • Ramen Heads and Jiro Dreams of Sushi: food-craft macro B-roll and workspace establishers. The gold standard for the craft strand.
  • Tampopo: kitchen choreography and eating close-ups.
  • Exit 8: subway corridor symmetry and one-point perspective.
  • Your Name: the Yotsuya and Suga Shrine establishers, shot at the real places.
  • Jujutsu Kaisen: Shibuya and Harajuku establishers at real, walkable locations.
  • 13 Assassins and Rurouni Kenshin: The Beginning: how action is covered, framed, and paced. Study the coverage, not the violence.
  • Rental Family: observational Tokyo street style.
  • Outrage: Kitano's locked-off, symmetrical wide shots. Study how a static frame makes a room feel charged before anything happens in it.
  • Ju-On: The Grudge: domestic interiors framed through doorways, hallways, and staircases. Useful any time your documentary steps inside a home or a shop.

Project Development Stages

I. Pre-Production

  • Group Formation Choose your team (groups of 3) and pick your subject: place or craft.
  • Greenlight Process Pitch your subject and production plan to an instructor (your producer). Approval is required to move forward.
  • Deliverables Submit a shot list with your 7 planned recreations clearly flagged, plus interview questions if your doc uses interviews or voiceover.

II. Production

  • Filming Shoot the documentary footage and the recreations. Aim for 10x footage relative to runtime. Record room tone everywhere you shoot.
  • Audio Discipline Clean interview sound beats pretty pictures. A documentary the audience cannot hear is a slideshow.

III. Post-Production

The Basics of Editing

A story is made three times: once during writing, again during filming, and finally during editing. Editing lets you manipulate the time and telling of the story. It is the final piece of the filmmaking process (and the one where all your earlier decisions come home to roost).

Setting Up a Project

Your timeline settings are not a creative decision. They are a match. Set the project to whatever you actually shot, at the highest quality your footage and your software will carry, and then get on with the edit.

Do not pick numbers here because you read them somewhere. Frame rate belongs to the camera, not the timeline, because it is bound to your shutter speed, and that decision was made before you ever opened the edit. Change it in post and the software has to invent frames or throw them away. (Footage that stutters for no visible reason is usually a timeline arguing with its source files.) Shoot it, then match it.

Basic Types of Edits

J Cuts and L Cuts

A J cut happens when the audio for a clip begins before the video does. You hear the next scene before you see it.

An L cut happens when the video for a clip begins before the audio does. You see the next scene while still hearing the last one.

(The names come from the shapes the clips make on your timeline. Look for them.)

Jump Cuts

A sudden jump from one clip to another, used to show fast-paced or heavy action. In documentary, jump cuts are also the standard tool for compressing an interview down to its best moments.

Cutaways

When a video clip is spliced in over another clip's audio, kind of like interrupting the main action to show something the person is talking about. Your subject says "I make the broth every morning," and we cut away to the broth.

Parallel Editing

Alternating between two scenes that are happening at different locations or times. Also called cross-cutting.

Transitions

Dissolves fade from one clip to another.

Fade to black starts on a clip and fades into a black screen.

Fade from black starts on a black screen and fades into a clip.

Wipes happen when a clip comes on screen from any direction and replaces the previous one.

During your process, look at the types of edits your film uses, and think about why. What is the scene trying to convey by using these? This is the same analysis habit you built with your Shot-Match Sheets: first understand what the technique is doing, then use it on purpose.

Color and Lighting

A large part of continuity is color. Watch the way scenes are colored and how bright they are. If the lighting or the color of lighting changes too much within the same setting in a scene, it can break the immersion and flow of the story. Ways to fix this are to adjust a clip's contrast, or add color filters to try and make clips match. Note: not every clip needs this, just those shot within the same setting.

Audio

Audio is also a very important part of continuity. You want viewers to be able to hear the audio, but not so loud it can cause hearing damage or just makes it hard to listen to. The fix is your audio mixer tool: use it to adjust the volume levels across clips so nothing spikes and nothing disappears.

Things to Watch Out For

Black holes: random black frames or gaps around clips.

Flash frames: random frames of leftover video around clips.

Sync: make sure video and audio tracks line up properly.

Continuity: make sure things line up from one clip to another (aka avoiding visual jumps).

Educational Goals

  • Composition Fluency Read a frame, name what it is doing, and rebuild it in the real city.
  • Editorial Judgment Two cuts from one footage pool force real decisions about what a story needs.
  • Documentary Voice Find a subject you love and make the audience love it too.
  • Portfolio Development Finish with two polished films and seven annotated shot studies.

Evaluation Criteria

Weighted and published up front. Process is graded, not just product.

  • Shot-Match Sheets and recreation craft (30%): analysis quality and fidelity of the 7 recreations
  • Editing craft (25%): the two-cut comparison and required techniques in evidence
  • Documentary voice (20%): subject clarity, access, and audio quality
  • Process (15%): pitch, flagged shot list, checkpoints hit
  • Reflection (10%): growth demonstrated, editing vocabulary used correctly

Final Submission Requirements

What to Submit

  • The (00:03:00) Cut: ends at (00:03:00). MP4 at the highest quality you can export, titles and credits included.
  • The (00:01:30) Cut: ends at (00:01:30). Same footage pool, must stand alone.
  • Shot-Match Sheets: minimum 7, PDF or images.
  • Pre-Production Pack: pitch, shot list with recreations flagged, interview questions if applicable.
  • Production Materials: dailies and B-roll (aim for 10x footage relative to runtime).
  • Self-Evaluation: written, oral, or video. Must name the editing techniques you used and why.

Collaborative Requirement

Each student must assist another group’s documentary for at least two hours (as crew, interview subject, or extra hands). Include proof (photos, logs) in your submission.

Extra Credit

Behind-the-Scenes Reel: an MP4 at the highest quality you can export, highlighting your production process, funny moments, and outtakes (integrated into credits or separate).

"Play the Infinite Game"

There is no perfect documentary, only the next interesting choice. Pick a subject you would visit anyway, steal your shots from filmmakers who already solved the problem, and let the edit teach you what your story is. Your final product is a record of that game.
